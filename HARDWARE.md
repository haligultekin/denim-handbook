## 1. Rivets: The Architecture of Seam Reinforcement

Rivets are the metal studs punched through high-stress points (pocket corners, fly bases, and crotch seams) to prevent seams from tearing out under mechanical tension.

- **Copper Burrs (Washer Rivets):** The classic 19th-century heavy-duty setup. A solid copper nail or tack is pushed through the fabric from the reverse side, an open copper washer (the burr) is fitted over the protruding post, and the excess stem is hammered flat. On early pairs, a tiny tuft of cotton denim was intentionally left poking through the center hole ("punch-through" rivets).
- **Early Patent Stamps (1870s–1880s):** The earliest surviving 1880s waist overalls feature copper rivets stamped *"Pat May 1872, LS&Co SF"*—referencing the original patent filing date rather than the eventual 1873 grant date.
- **Hidden Rivets (1937–1966):** By the 1930s, consumers, automobile drivers, school boards, and horsemen complained that exposed rear copper rivets were scratching wooden chairs, church pews, car upholstery, and leather saddles. In 1937, Levi's factory superintendent M. Granborn engineered a way to conceal the rivets *under* the folded top corners of the back pocket lining. The rivets were still there for strength, but hidden from sight! By 1966, hidden rivets were phased out entirely because the stiff copper burrs eventually ground through the denim from the inside out.
- **Rodeo Ben's Flat Rivet (1947):** When celebrity cowboy tailor Bernard Lichtenstein ("Rodeo Ben") designed the 11MW for Wrangler, rodeo cowboys complained about domed rivets snagging on ropes and scratching expensive saddles. Ben engineered completely flat, mirror-smooth copper rivets that sat flush against the fabric, establishing an industry benchmark for Western equestrian jeans.
- **Bar Tacks:** Beginning in the late 1960s, heavy industrial zig-zag thread stitching (bar tacks sewn on machines like the Juki LK-1854) replaced back pocket rivets. Bar tacks lay completely flat against the fabric, eliminate all risk of scratching surfaces, and withstand tremendous lateral pull.

## 2. Tack Buttons: Waistband Closures and Shank Mechanics

You won't find flat, sewn-on dress shirt buttons on a proper pair of jeans. Denim uses two-piece metal tack buttons that punch through multiple layers of heavy waistband denim and lock onto a barbed post.

- **The Keyhole Eyelet:** Heavy metal buttons require clearance for the button stem or shank. Specialized buttonhole machines (like the Reece 101) punch a teardrop-shaped "keyhole" eyelet at the end of the buttonhole slit. This gives the tack button room to pivot naturally without puckering or pulling the waistband seam out of alignment.
- **WWII "Donut" Buttons (1942–1945):** Under US War Production Board Order L-85, garment manufacturers were forced to drastically conserve metal. Brands eliminated solid-face buttons in favor of open-center "donut" buttons, significantly reducing the amount of raw brass and zinc used per garment.
- **"Laurel Wreath" Buttons:** Another iconic wartime relic. To eliminate brand-specific metal stamping dies, manufacturers were required to purchase generic government-approved buttons stamped with a universal laurel wreath (symbolizing Allied victory).
- **Solid Brass & Pewter Tack Buttons:** Post-war and modern heritage makers use solid cast or stamped brass, iron, and copper buttons. As raw denim ages, these buttons oxidize naturally, shedding their outer finish to develop a unique patina alongside fading indigo.

## 3. The Fly Closure: Button vs. Zip

The choice between a button fly and a zipper fly changes how jeans fit, drape, and evolve after washing.

- **The Button Fly (The Raw Denim Standard):** The undisputed champion for raw, unsanforized denim. Because loomstate cotton shrinks unevenly (up to 10%) when washed, a button fly has individual connection points that flex, shift, and settle naturally with the shrinking fabric.
- **The Zip Fly (The Modern Utility Standard):** Introduced to workwear in 1926 by H.D. Lee on the 101Z cowboy pant (originally utilizing Hookless Fastener mechanisms). Zippers allowed cowboys to zip up with cold, gloved hands on the open range.
  - *The Shrink-to-Fit Warning:* Never put a zipper fly on unsanforized denim! When the jeans hit hot water, the cotton shrinks drastically, but the woven polyester/cotton zipper tape does not shrink at the same rate. This creates permanent "wave" buckling down the front rise. Always ensure denim is Sanforized before installing a zipper.
- **Historic Zipper Makers:** The golden era of workwear zippers was defined by **Talon** (especially the iconic brass Talon 42), **Scovill Gripper**, and **Crown**. Today, Japanese giant **YKK**, along with heritage reproduction specialists like **Universal** and **Waldes**, recreate vintage brass and nickel zipper teeth with period-correct locking sliders.

## 4. Supplementary Fasteners: Cinch Buckles and Suspender Buttons

- **Buckle-Back Cinches:** Found on pre-1940s waist overalls. A small horizontal strap with a pronged metal buckle at the rear waistband allowed wearers to adjust fit without a belt. Early buckles featured sharp two-prong steel teeth that pierced directly through the fabric strap; later versions adopted safer slide-buckle designs.
- **Suspender (Brace) Buttons:** Before belt loops were introduced in 1922, waist overalls were held up exclusively with suspenders. Six metal tack buttons (four in front, two in back) were riveted directly along the waistband perimeter. Levi's continued including suspender buttons alongside belt loops until 1937, when customers began cutting them off with wire cutters!
