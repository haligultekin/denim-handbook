# The Nuts & Bolts: Hardware and Fasteners

Think of hardware as the structural foundation of your jeans. Because early workwear took a massive beating, standard cotton thread just wasn't enough to hold the seams together under stress. This problem actually led to one of the most famous inventions in clothing history: the copper rivet.

## Rivets: Holding It All Together
Rivets are the tiny metal studs punched into the high-stress points of your jeans (like the corners of the pockets and the crotch) to keep the fabric from tearing apart.

- **Copper Burrs (Washer Rivets):** This is the classic, heavy-duty setup. A copper nail is pushed through the fabric, a washer (the burr) is placed over it, and the remaining bit of the nail is hammered flat to lock it all in place. 
- **Hidden Rivets:** Back in 1937, people started complaining that exposed copper rivets on their back pockets were scratching up their horse saddles and furniture! To fix this, brands started hiding the rivets *under* the top layer of denim on the back pockets. 
- **Bar Tacks:** By the 1960s, a new technique took over. Thick, zig-zag stitching—known as a bar tack—mostly replaced hidden rivets. It was cheaper to produce, laid flatter on the fabric, and still offered great reinforcement for those back pockets.

## Tack Buttons
You won't find normal, sew-on buttons on a good pair of jeans. Instead, denim uses metal buttons that literally punch through the fabric and lock into place with a metal tack on the back.

- **"Donut" Buttons:** These buttons have a hole right through the middle, making them look like a donut. You'll often see these on World War II-era jeans (like the 1944 LVC 501). They were designed this way to save precious metal for the war effort!
- **"Laurel Wreath" Buttons:** Another fascinating detail from WWII. Because the US government forced companies to use standardized parts to speed up production, many brands had to use generic buttons stamped with a laurel wreath—a classic symbol of victory.

## The Great Debate: Button vs. Zip Fly
The type of closure you choose for the crotch of your jeans changes how they fit and feel, especially after their first wash.

- **The Button Fly:** The undisputed king for raw denim. Because raw, unsanforized denim shrinks and stretches a lot as you break it in, a button fly offers the flexibility needed to move with the fabric as it changes shape. 
- **The Zip Fly:** Zippers became hugely popular in the 1950s, largely thanks to brands like Lee. 
   - *A Quick Warning on Shrink-to-Fit:* Putting a zipper on unsanforized (shrink-to-fit) denim is a recipe for disaster. When you wash the jeans, the cotton will shrink up to 10%, but the metal zipper tape stays exactly the same size. The result? A weird, bumpy, buckling zipper line down the front of your jeans. If you're going unsanforized, always stick to buttons!
   - *The Hardware Makers:* Historically, **Talon** and **Scovill** were the big names making zippers in America. Today, **YKK** (and their vintage reproduction brand, Universal) rules the industry, along with specialized vintage reproduction makers like **Waldes**.
